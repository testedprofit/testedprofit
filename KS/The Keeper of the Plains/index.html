Keeper of the Plains

The Mid-America All-Indian Museum says the Keeper is a 44-foot-tall, 5-ton Cor-Ten statue, constructed in 1974, and the vision of Blackbear Bosin, who helped establish the museum. Visit Wichita calls it a five-ton, 44-foot-tall steel sculpture by the Native American artist Blackbear Bosin, standing in downtown Wichita where the Big and Little Arkansas rivers join. The Kansas Historical Society says the same height and says Francis Blackbear Bosin donated the steel figure of an American Indian to the city. Wikipedia calls it a 13.4-metre, or 44-foot, Cor-Ten steel sculpture by the Kiowa-Comanche artist, and its infobox lists the material as weathering steel and the height as 44 feet. The pages agree on 44 feet. Wikipedia also says the city and private organizations commissioned it to mark the United States Bicentennial, and that it was erected in 1974.

The historical society says Bosin was born on 5 June 1921 in Anadarko, Oklahoma, of Kiowa-Comanche heritage, came to Wichita in 1940, and died in Wichita on 9 August 1980. Visit Wichita says he designed the figure with hands raised in supplication to the Great Spirit, to honor the Indigenous people of the land between the two rivers, which that page calls sacred ground. Wikipedia places the sculpture at the confluence of the Arkansas and Little Arkansas rivers, adjacent to the Mid-America All-Indian Center.

The museum says that in May 2007, at the end of an eight-year, 20-million-dollar project, the Keeper was placed on a 30-foot promontory at the confluence of the Little and Big Arkansas rivers. Wikipedia says a spring and summer 2006 project elevated the sculpture on a 30-foot rock promontory. Both dates stay. The museum says the plaza has yucca, medicinal herbs, and cactus, and displays on Plains Indian customs, including the four elements of the medicine wheel: air, fire, water, and earth.

Visit Wichita calls the nightly lighting the Ring of Fire: five fire drums burn for 15 minutes, from 9:00 to 9:15 p.m. during daylight saving time and from 7:00 to 7:15 p.m. during standard time. High wind, rain, or other bad weather can cancel it. The museum says the five drums are lit for 15 minutes at 7:00 p.m. in winter and 9:00 p.m. in summer. Wikipedia calls the pits the Rings of Fire, says they are lit by hand in 15-minute increments, and says they are generally lit once a night around 7 p.m. in winter and at sunset in summer. The 9:00 p.m. summer time and the sunset wording are both kept.

Visit Wichita says the statue can be visited between 5 a.m. and midnight by crossing one of two footbridges, one from each side of the river. The plaza is free. By day, visitors can also enter the plaza from the Mid-America All-Indian Museum, which Visit Wichita describes as ticketed. The museum’s own page gives its address as 650 N Seneca, Wichita, Kansas 67203. The City of Wichita page that the museum points to did not open for this check, so it is named and not linked. These pages do not describe bow-and-arrow bridge designs or a named speaker at a 1974 dedication.

Questions

Who made the Keeper, and how tall is it?

The Mid-America All-Indian Museum, Visit Wichita, and the Kansas Historical Society all call it a 44-foot steel sculpture by Blackbear Bosin. The museum adds that it weighs 5 tons and is Cor-Ten steel, constructed in 1974.

When do the fires burn?

Visit Wichita says five fire drums burn for 15 minutes, at 9:00 to 9:15 p.m. during daylight saving time and 7:00 to 7:15 p.m. during standard time, unless weather cancels them. Wikipedia says the pits are generally lit around 7 p.m. in winter and at sunset in summer.
