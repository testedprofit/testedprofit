The Dancing Mary in Portland

The Dancing Mary, also called Moving Mary, is the Consolatrix Afflictorum statue at Mount Calvary Cemetery, 333 SW Skyline Boulevard, Portland, Oregon 97221. The cemetery is a Catholic hilltop in the West Hills, owned by the Archdiocese of Portland. Local folklore says the figure has been seen dancing or moving late at night. The carved title means Mary, comforter of the afflicted. Come during the posted visitation hours, in daylight, and leave before dusk.

Mount Calvary on SW Skyline

The Dancing Mary stands in Mount Calvary Cemetery at 333 SW Skyline Boulevard, Portland, Oregon 97221. The cemetery sits in the West Hills, on the ridge locals call Skyline. The Archdiocese of Portland owns it. It was established in 1888 and covers more than 100 acres, with ground burial, a green burial section, cremation, and a mausoleum. From the hill you can see north, east, and west, toward the Columbia, the Cascades, and the Coast Range. The office phone is 503-292-6621, and the staff email is mstaff@ccpdxor.com. The grounds are five minutes from downtown and still feel set apart from the street grid below.

Visitation is open 365 days a year. In winter the hours are 7:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. In summer they are 7:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. The cemetery closes the grounds at dusk because the hill has little lighting. Winter hours can change. In a storm, call before you drive up. The cemetery often follows Portland Public Schools closures, and because Mount Calvary is higher than the city it may close without a separate notice. The office is open Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., by appointment only. Holiday office closures include New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents' Day, Good Friday, Independence Day, Labor Day, Veterans Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas. Christmas Eve the office closes at noon. Memorial Day the office is by appointment. The grounds themselves follow the seasonal visitation clock, and they still close at dusk.

Treat the visit as a cemetery, not a night dare. Stay inside the boundaries. Walking directions sometimes suggest a path that leaves the grounds. Use the cemetery lanes. Fresh flowers are welcome on outdoor graves and are removed when they no longer look fresh. Pots wider than six inches, glass, wires, pins, artificial flowers, photographs, souvenirs, and wreaths are kept off the graves except on holidays the cemetery names. Families do not plant their own trees or flowers. Only the cemetery plants and maintains the landscape. Deer come through and eat arrangements. Do not climb The Dancing Mary, and do not climb other monuments. Sound carries off this hill. Leave it quiet, read Consolatrix Afflictorum, and go before the gate hour.

The figure and the stories

Portland's Dancing Mary is a cemetery sculpture, not a stage. Bill Natkin's account, published October 12, 2024, and tagged Portland, Oregon, places her on a hilltop cemetery and records the inscription Consolatrix Afflictorum on the figure. He notes that local folklore has used the names Dancing Mary and Moving Mary for a long time. In the versions he sets down, people say the statue has been seen dancing or moving late at night. Some tales go further and say she haunts visitors.

The hill explains part of the reputation. Natkin lives close enough to know the grounds draw people after dark, and he notes that sound travels well from the hilltop. His own observation of those late visits is beer, smoke, and fireworks, not a moving statue. A story that starts with a dare on a loud hill can outlive the night that invented it. The photograph he published that day shows the cemetery sculpture the stories name. What you can verify in daylight is the figure and the Latin. What you cannot verify is a dance.

Treat the legend as folklore. Repeat it if you like, and do not treat it as a reason to enter a cemetery after the gates are meant to be still. The people buried on that hill are the reason the statue is there. A roadside landmark can be famous and still be a graveyard.

What Consolatrix Afflictorum means

Consolatrix Afflictorum is Latin for comforter of the afflicted. In English the title is Our Lady of Consolation, or Mary, Comforter of the Afflicted. The Catholic Church gives this title to the Virgin Mary. It is one of the invocations in the Litany of Loreto. The devotion is Augustinian in origin. Along with Saints Augustine and Monica, Our Lady of Consolation is one of the three patrons of the Augustinian orders. By the early 18th century, asking for a final blessing before death in her name was a widespread custom.

That history fits a cemetery statue better than a ghost story does. A figure labeled comforter of the afflicted stands where people bury their dead and come back to mourn. The Latin is a prayer, not a nickname. Dancing Mary and Moving Mary are what Portland mouths added later. When you find the statue, read the carved words first. They tell you what the people who set the figure meant. The night stories tell you what later visitors wanted the stone to do.

Our Lady of Consolation is venerated in more than one country. The oldest icon under a consolation title is associated with Turin and, in later telling, with Saint Luke. Shrines and pilgrimages use the same invocation. The Portland statue is a local example of that wider title: a cemetery Mary whose Latin name is the Litany's comforter, and whose English fame is a legend about movement. You can hold both facts. The Church's title is documented. The dance is a story Portland tells.

Marian art and the inscription

A Marian statue is identified by its title. Painters, sculptors, and cemetery workshops have made thousands of figures of Mary. The hands, the color of the robe, and the child on her arm can change from one workshop to the next. The name tells you which devotion the figure belongs to. On The Dancing Mary that name is carved: Consolatrix Afflictorum. English speakers say Our Lady of Consolation, or Mary, Comforter of the Afflicted. Portland's spoken names, Dancing Mary and Moving Mary, are not on the stone. They are the folklore that grew up around a night visit. If you are learning how to read Catholic outdoor sculpture, start with the letters, then the setting, then the stories people tell.

The title is Augustinian. The friars of Saint Augustine spread the devotion, and Our Lady of Consolation is one of the three patrons of the Augustinian orders, with Augustine and his mother, Monica. In Augustinian houses the usual picture shows Mary holding the Child Jesus on her lap, and both of them holding the Augustinian cincture, the leather belt of the order. The Archconfraternity of Our Lady of Consolation, also called the Archconfraternity of the Cincture, grew out of a faculty the order received in 1439 to enroll lay people. A confraternity of Our Lady of Consolation was founded in Bologna in 1495. In 1575 it joined the cincture confraternity. Members wore a black leather belt, fasted on the vigil of Saint Augustine, and said a short rosary of thirteen Our Fathers and thirteen Hail Marys, then the Hail Holy Queen. The feast of the archconfraternity is September 4, which is also the date the Order of Saint Augustine keeps. Other orders keep other days: the Conventual Franciscans on May 25, the Benedictines on July 5. In the United States the Saturday after the feast of Saint Augustine, August 28, is used. None of that calendar is painted on the Portland figure. It is the history behind the two Latin words.

Consolatrix Afflictorum is also a line of the Litany of Loreto, the long public prayer that addresses Mary by many titles. A litany works by accumulation. Each name is a short claim: refuge of sinners, help of Christians, comforter of the afflicted. A cemetery is one of the places that last claim is easiest to see. People come back to a grave with flowers, and a figure labeled comforter of the afflicted stands in the same ground. By the early 18th century, asking for a final blessing before death in the name of Our Lady of Consolation was a common custom. That is a deathbed prayer, not a ghost story. It explains why a cemetery, rather than a plaza, is a natural home for this title.

The same invocation has traveled. The oldest icon under a consolation title is associated with Turin and, in later telling, with Saint Luke. In 1652 Pope Innocent X encouraged a French confraternity of Our Lady, Comforter of the Afflicted. In 1856 Pope Pius IX granted a canonical coronation to an image at Verdelais, and in 1924 Pope Pius XI raised that shrine to a minor basilica. In England, the shrine at West Grinstead dates from 1876 and was the first shrine of Mary founded there after the Reformation. Pope Leo XIII granted its image a coronation carried out in 1893. In Belgium, a 1535 Pieta in Leuven, long called Our Lady of Fever because people asked it for healing, was renamed Our Lady of Consolation of the Afflicted. Pope Pius X granted it a coronation in 1907. Kevelaer in Germany venerates Our Lady, Consoler of the Afflicted, from a 1642 engraving of Our Lady of Luxembourg. Pope Leo XIII crowned that image in 1892, and Pope John Paul II visited in 1987. These are church shrines with their own images. The Portland statue is not one of them. It is a local cemetery figure that uses the same Latin title.

Reading one is a small skill. Look for the language first. Latin on a Catholic monument usually names a devotion, a saint, or a person buried nearby. Consolatrix is a noun, comforter. Afflictorum is a plural, of the afflicted. Together they are a title, not a sentence about the statue moving. Then look at the material and the site. Mount Calvary is a working Catholic cemetery, established in 1888, with more than a century of family monuments around any single figure. A Marian statue in that company is a devotional marker for people who are mourning, which is what the Litany line is for. The folklore names come last. Dancing Mary and Moving Mary tell you what Portland has said about the figure after dark. They do not replace the inscription, and they do not change the hours. The cemetery closes at dusk. The legend is a story to repeat in daylight, on the path, with the Latin still readable.

How to visit without turning it into a stunt

Go in daylight. A hilltop cemetery is readable then: the figure, the inscription, the paths, the view. After dark the same hill carries sound, and the folklore is an invitation some people take as a party. Natkin's account of late visitors is drinking, smoking, and fireworks. That is a poor use of a graveyard, and it is a poor way to see a statue whose whole inscription is about comfort. If a gate is closed, stay out. If the grounds are open, walk, read Consolatrix Afflictorum, and leave the monuments as you found them.

Do not climb the figure for a photograph. A crow may perch on her, as one 2024 picture shows, but you are not a bird. Keep off the stone and off the graves around it. The roadside quality is the point for a passing look: you can find her from the cemetery lanes without a ticketed museum. Give the visit the length of a careful walk, not a hunt for a moving shadow.

Portland has other public figures if you are making a day of statues. Joan of Arc and Portlandia are city sculptures with their own streets and their own histories. The Dancing Mary stays the cemetery one. Farther east, the Wildland Firefighter Monument in Prineville is a different Oregon memorial, a bronze for fire crews rather than a Marian title. Questions

Where is The Dancing Mary?

In Mount Calvary Cemetery at 333 SW Skyline Blvd, Portland, OR 97221, a Catholic hilltop cemetery in the West Hills. The figure is also called Moving Mary. The carved name is Consolatrix Afflictorum.

What name is on the statue?

The inscription is Consolatrix Afflictorum. That Latin title means Mary, Comforter of the Afflicted, also called Our Lady of Consolation. Local folklore calls the same figure the Dancing Mary or Moving Mary.

Does the statue actually dance?

The Portland stories say she has been seen dancing or moving late at night, and some versions say she haunts visitors. Those are folklore. The words cut on the figure are a prayer for comfort, not a claim that the stone moves.

When should I go?

The cemetery is open 365 days a year, 7:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. in winter and 7:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. in summer, and the grounds close at dusk. Call 503-292-6621 if a storm is coming. Mt. Calvary sits higher than the city and may close without notice. Stay on the paths, do not climb the statue, and leave the graves alone.
