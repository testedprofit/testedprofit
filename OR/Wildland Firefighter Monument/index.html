Wildland Firefighter Monument in Prineville

The Wildland Firefighter Monument is a bronze of three firefighters in Prineville, Oregon, at the west end of Ochoco Creek Park. Sculptor David R. Nelson of Marble, Colorado, made it for a dedication on June 15, 1996. It honors wildland crews of every year, and it remembers the Prineville Hotshots who died on Storm King Mountain.

The bronze and who made it

On June 9, 1996, The Spokesman-Review reported that a bronze of three firefighters gathered around a charred tree had been loaded onto a truck in Denver for a 1,200-mile trip to Prineville. The dedication was set for June 15, and the statue was the centerpiece of a monument to all wildland firefighters. Sculptor David Nelson of Marble, Colorado, was 44. He was chosen from 70 artists. A committee of parents of Prineville Hotshots commissioned the work.

In a July 5, 2014 Post Independent interview, Nelson described the three figures. He had been a finalist for the Glenwood Springs memorial and was selected the next year for Prineville, home to nine of the 14. He interviewed relatives of those who died and survivors who had been on Storm King Mountain. His bronze shows three hotshot crew members on the ground at a fire: a woman, a Caucasian man, and a Hispanic man. The sawyer is cutting a tree, and a feather hangs from that helmet for the many Native American firefighters on federal crews. Nelson wanted the piece to be for all wildland firefighters, living and deceased, and to show how young the people were who died. The Storm King 14 ranged from their early 20s to their mid-40s.

The bronze is one-quarter larger than life and sits on a 3-foot stone base. It measures 14 feet long, 8 feet wide, and 11 feet high. The firefighters wear clothing and carry equipment from the 1994 season. Plaques set in stone line the walkway and tell about the nine young firefighters from Prineville.

Storm King Mountain, July 6, 1994

The fire is the South Canyon Fire on Storm King Mountain, near Glenwood Springs, Colorado. On July 6, 1994, 14 federal wildland firefighters died there. Nine were Prineville Hotshots. Two were Idaho-based smokejumpers, including Jim Thrash of New Meadows. Terri Hagen and Roger Roth of the Prineville crew were Native American, both from the Onondaga tribe of the Iroquois Nation. Jon Kelso was another of the Prineville Hotshots who died. His parents, Marv and Anita Kelso, later looked after the monument in town.

The parents' committee put the memorial in Prineville so the Hotshots' hometown would have a place for every wildland firefighter, not a marker only for 1994. Ochoco National Forest spokesman Norm Hesseldahl told The Spokesman-Review that part of the $120,000 cost would be paid with $72,000 in donations collected after the deaths, and that the parents voted to put that money toward the monument. The committee also planned to raise more by selling gallery-sized replicas and figurines.

Glenwood Springs has its own memorial, and it is a different bronze. Arizona artist Joyce Killebrew made the centerpiece at Two Rivers Park: a smokejumper, a woman hotshot for the four women who died, and a helitack crew member. That sculpture was dedicated on July 6, 1995. Fourteen boulders there carry biographies of the dead. If you are looking up the Storm King 14, the Glenwood monument names the whole crew. The Prineville monument is the one David Nelson made for the Oregon town that sent nine of them.

What the monument teaches

Stand at the bronze and you are looking at a wildland crew, not a single portrait. Hotshots are the federal hand crews that hike into wildfires with hand tools and saws. The sawyer in the group is the firefighter cutting a tree. The charred trunk, the 1994 clothing, and the equipment on their bodies are the job as it looked in the season of the South Canyon Fire. The feather on the helmet is there because Native American firefighters have long served on those federal crews, and because Hagen and Roth of the Prineville Hotshots died on that mountain.

The walkway is the other half of the lesson. Stone plaques lead up to the bronze and remember the nine from Prineville. The monument's charge, from the parents who commissioned it and from Nelson, is wider than that list. It is for wildland firefighters past, present, and future: the people who still take the same kind of assignment. A short visit can hold both facts at once. Nine neighbors from this town died on July 6, 1994, and the bronze faces the crews that come after them.

The park around the monument is ordinary public ground, which is part of the point. Crook County Parks and Recreation describes Ochoco Creek Park as 14.85 acres along Ochoco Creek, one of the larger parks in the district, and points visitors to the monuments at the west end. You do not buy a ticket. You walk in during park hours, read the plaques, and look at the three figures. Families who want a longer stop can use the rest of the park along the creek after they have been to the bronze.

Hours, parking, and a careful visit

Ochoco Creek Park, at 455 Juniper St, is open from dawn to dusk. The Wildland Firefighter Monument is at the west end, beside Northeast Elm Street, inside the park. There is no admission fee, and you can visit through the year during park hours. Use the park parking and walk to the west end. The pin 44.30442, -120.84146 is the memorial.

Give the bronze room. In August 2018 the Central Oregonian reported that vandals had broken off a chainsaw blade and a handheld radio cord. Neighbors raised more than $16,000, past a $15,000 goal, through a fund Sam Pearcy of the Ochoco National Forest opened. The Crook County Parks and Recreation Foundation, which had taken over special care of the monument from Marv and Anita Kelso, planned a repair and a full refurbishing, plus bollards and a chain. Parks supervisor Larry Penington asked Nelson about the repair. Nelson pointed the district to a forge closer to home, and the district found Tim Parks of Enterprise to make the parts and weld them back on. The pieces on the figures are part of the memorial. Leave them in place.

Questions

What is the Wildland Firefighter Monument?

It is David R. Nelson's bronze of three firefighters gathered around a charred tree, at the west end of Ochoco Creek Park in Prineville, Oregon. The dedication was June 15, 1996. The monument honors all wildland firefighters and remembers the 14 who died on Storm King Mountain on July 6, 1994, including nine Prineville Hotshots.

Who made it?

Sculptor David R. Nelson of Marble, Colorado. In 1996 he was 44 and had been chosen from 70 artists. The bronze shows three hotshot crew members: a woman, a Caucasian man, and a Hispanic man. A feather hangs from the sawyer's helmet.

Who commissioned it?

A committee of parents of Prineville Hotshots commissioned the statue. Nine of the 14 firefighters who died at Storm King were from that crew. The 1996 cost was $120,000, with $72,000 from donations collected after the deaths.

How do I visit the monument?

Go to the west end of Ochoco Creek Park, 455 Juniper St, Prineville, OR 97754, beside Northeast Elm Street. The park is open from dawn to dusk. There is no admission fee. The pin is 44.30442, -120.84146.

What happened at Storm King?

On July 6, 1994, 14 federal wildland firefighters died on the South Canyon Fire on Storm King Mountain near Glenwood Springs, Colorado. Nine were Prineville Hotshots. A separate bronze for all 14 stands at Two Rivers Park in Glenwood Springs. The Prineville monument is the one in the Hotshots' hometown.
