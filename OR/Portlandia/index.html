Portland Building, downtown

Portlandia

Portlandia is Raymond Kaskey's hammered-copper figure kneeling above the entrance of the Portland Building. She is the second largest copper repoussé statue in the United States, after the Statue of Liberty. The city installed her on 6 October 1985. This is an independent Profitnet guide to the sculpture.

See her

From the street
1120 SW Fifth Avenue, Portland. She is about three stories above the entrance, facing a narrow, tree-lined block. The sidewalk view is free, any hour.
Closer
The building's first and second floors are open to the public Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. A balcony on the second floor was built so you can get nearer the statue. A smaller 3-D print stands at the top of those stairs.
Photos
Personal photographs are welcome. Commercial use of the image needs Raymond Kaskey's permission. He kept the copyright.

Lady Commerce, in copper

The figure follows the woman on the city seal of that time, Lady Commerce. She wears classical clothes, holds a trident in her left hand, and reaches down with her right. Kaskey modeled her on his wife, and he wanted a stronger figure than the other competition entries. The city held that competition in 1981. Michael Graves, the architect of the postmodern Portland Building, had pictured a large Lady Commerce on the new offices. In 1982 the arts commission, with public comment, chose Kaskey's design.

She is 34 feet 10 inches high and weighs 6.5 short tons, about 13,000 pounds. Standing, the city reconstruction account says, she would be 50 feet tall. The copper was hammered, at least 50 strokes to the square inch, until the sheets were thin, then riveted. The preservation architects who later scaffolded her describe a wooden frame inside that skin, and they call the whole thing fragile. The Portland Tribune's 2015 fact box instead listed a steel frame under the copper. What you see from the sidewalk is the hammered copper.

Kaskey and his assistant Michael Lasell built the sections in a Maryland suburb of Washington, D.C., and shipped them. Gunderson's barge yard on the Willamette assembled the figure. On 6 October 1985 she came up the river on a barge and was lifted onto the building. Thousands watched. Mayor Bud Clark, newly elected, paddled out to meet her. A plaque carries Ronald Talney's poem, also called Portlandia:

She kneels down, and from the quietness of copper reaches out. We take that stillness into ourselves, and somewhere deep in the earth our breath becomes her city. If she could speak this is what she would say: Follow that breath. Home is the journey we make. This is how the world knows where we are.

The copyright he kept

The work was paid for largely with public art money, and Kaskey still owns the copyright. Willamette Week reported $228,000 in public funds and about $100,000 more in private gifts. The Tribune's 2015 notes listed a $198,000 city arts commission and about $100,000 in private donations. Early in the contract the city held the rights. When Kaskey wanted to hand out a postcard of the unfinished figure, the city attorney said that broke the contract. In March 1985 the contract was changed: the copyright went back to Kaskey, and the city kept a limited right to use the image for its own publicity. The Metropolitan Arts Commission, now the Regional Arts and Culture Council, had just adopted a policy that let artists keep copyright in work the public bought.

He has threatened suits over unlicensed commercial pictures and drawings, and he has said he will not sell the rights to the city. In 2012 Laurelwood Brewing put an illustration on Portlandia Pils and later paid a cash settlement. The television series Portlandia used the statue in its titles only after long talks, with a condition that the figure not be used in a disparaging way. Personal photographs, including the ones the arts council invites people to tag, are a different matter from a product, a poster, or an ad.

Covered, then back in view

The Portland Building, finished in 1982, leaked, and its systems were old. In 2015 the council ordered a reconstruction for water, operations, and seismic work. Moving Portlandia off her ledge risked cracking the thin copper, so the architects left her in place and built scaffolding around her. She was out of public sight for more than a year. In 2019 the scaffolding came down. Conservators had gone over the copper, and the statue was rededicated as part of the rebuilt building. She was not taken off the facade.

A public reopening was set for 19 March 2020 and then postponed. The first and second floors are the public parts of the building now, open weekdays from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. From the sidewalk on Fifth Avenue you still get the long view, three stories up, between the trees. From the new balcony you get the nearer one. The small print by the second-floor stairs, made with Form 3D Foundry, is the version you can stand next to indoors.

Other figures

Joan of Arc is the other Portland bronze on this site, on a different street and from a different century. The Rosa Parks statue is in Eugene, at the bus plaza, not on this block. Portlandia stays the copper woman over the Portland Building door.

Questions

Where is Portlandia?

Above the entrance of the Portland Building at 1120 SW Fifth Avenue in downtown Portland. She is about three stories up. You can see her from the sidewalk. On weekdays, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., the public floors include a balcony for a closer look.

How tall is she, and when was she installed?

The statue is 34 feet 10 inches high and weighs 6.5 short tons. She was installed on 6 October 1985, after the sections came up the Willamette on a barge.

Can you use a photo of Portlandia commercially?

Raymond Kaskey kept the copyright. Personal photos are welcome. Commercial reproductions need his permission. In 2012 Laurelwood Brewing settled with him after putting the figure on a beer label.
