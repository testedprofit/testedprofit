The Quest, Oregon State

Alice E. Biddle statue

The Alice E. Biddle statue is a bronze called The Quest. Kirk St. Maur made it in 1983. It stands at the southwest corner of Strand Agriculture Hall, on the path from Memorial Union, on the Oregon State University campus in Corvallis. This is an independent Profitnet guide to that figure.

The bronze

She stands with her head turned to her right and a rolled paper, the diploma, in that hand. Both arms are held a little out from her sides. The bronze is about 5 feet 11 inches by 4 feet by 4 feet 6 inches. On the lower left back of the skirt the artist signed St. Maur, with a copyright mark and 1983. A marker nearby reads:

The Quest symbolizes Alice E. Biddle, who in 1870 became the first woman to graduate from what is now Oregon State University. It reflects the energy, dedication and strength of all students in the pursuit of learning.

The marker credits Oregon's Art in Public Places program. The Oregon Arts Commission commissioned the work through its 1 percent for Art in State Buildings program. Oregon State University administers the sculpture. In March 1993 the Smithsonian Save Outdoor Sculpture survey recorded the bronze as needing treatment. That note is the survey's record from that year.

The class of 1870

Alice E. Biddle finished the college course in three years and took her degree at 16. She was one of three people in Corvallis College's first graduating class, with Robert M. Veatch and James K. P. Currin. The university chronology calls those the first degrees granted in the western United States by a state-assisted college or university. At the commencement on 30 June 1870, Veatch gave the valedictory, Utility of Science. Biddle read a paper, Progress of Mind. A class photograph in the archives shows the three of them.

In December 1870 she married one of her professors, William W. Moreland. Campus writing credits her with organizing the first alumni reunion, and an Oregon Digital account remembered her as Corvallis's most intelligent young woman. Moreland, a faculty member in the 1860s and 1870s and clerk of the legislature, is the person credited with pressing the state to accept the Morrill Act and name Corvallis College the land-grant institution. In 1973 the old Forestry Building, built in 1917 at 2550 SW Jefferson Way, was named Moreland Hall for him. It now holds the School of Writing, Literature, and Film. The hall is a short walk from the statue, on the same Jefferson Way as Memorial Union.

The artist and the program

Kirk St. Maur, born in 1949, modeled the figure. The commission came from the state's percent-for-art rule on public buildings, the same program named on the marker. The statue has stood on that corner since 1983. A 2000 archives exhibit still places the sculpture of Alice Biddle at the southwest corner of Strand Agricultural Hall, which is the Strand Agriculture Hall on today's campus maps.

Other campus figures

The same path has company. A wooden Benny the Beaver, carved by Floyd Davis from a redwood and given to the class of 1966, stands inside the southwest entrance of Memorial Union. Martin Kukučín, a seated reader by Ivan Meštrović, is on the west side of the Valley Library. The Quest is the one between the union and Strand Hall. Off campus, the Rosa Parks statue is in Eugene, and Portlandia and the gilded Joan of Arc are in Portland.

Questions

Who does the Alice E. Biddle statue depict?

The Quest is Kirk St. Maur's 1983 bronze of Alice E. Biddle. She was 16 when she graduated in the class of 1870, the first woman, and one of the first three people, to take a degree from Corvallis College, the school that became Oregon State.

Where does the statue stand?

On the Oregon State campus in Corvallis, at the southwest corner of Strand Agriculture Hall, 170 SW Waldo Place, on the path from Memorial Union at 2501 SW Jefferson Way. It is outdoors. No ticket.

What did Biddle do after graduation?

At the 30 June 1870 commencement she read a paper, Progress of Mind. In December 1870 she married professor William W. Moreland. Moreland Hall, at 2550 SW Jefferson Way, was named for him in 1973.
