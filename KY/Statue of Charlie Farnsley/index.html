Charlie Farnsley on West Main

A life-size bronze of Mayor Charles Farnsley sits on an oversized bench on West Main Street in Louisville. The Louisville Commission on Public Art record PALM 00012 titles the work Charles Farnsley, names the artist Dawn D. Yates, dates it 1999, and lists the medium as metal, bronze. It says the sculpture was commissioned for the 50th anniversary of the Fund for the Arts, which he founded, and was dedicated in 1999 in front of the Fund for the Arts at 623 West Main Street.

The downtown listing

Louisville Downtown Partnership's art page for Mayor Charles P. Farnsley lists the artist as Dawn D. Yates, the site as the Fund for the Arts, the sponsor as Louisville Metro Government, and the address as 623 West Main Street. The page's map pin is at 38.257166, -85.760784. Yates holds a BFA from Murray State University and worked at the Bright Foundry. The public-art record credits her as the sculptor. It says she made the bench extra long because Farnsley was a relaxed man who would have invited people to sit and talk.

The mayor on the bench

The record identifies him as Charles Rowland Peaslee Farnsley, 1907 to 1990. He served in the Kentucky House from 1936 to 1940 and in the U.S. House from 1965 to 1967. He was mayor of Louisville from 1948 to 1953. Besides the Louisville Fund, later the Fund for the Arts, the record says he expanded the library and park systems, improved streets, and developed recreational facilities for children. It also says he was known for a black string tie and as a defender of the Confederacy, and that desegregation began during his time as mayor.

Questions

Who sculpted the Farnsley bronze, and when?

The Louisville public-art record PALM 00012 names the artist as Dawn D. Yates, dates the bronze 1999, and says it was dedicated that year in front of the Fund for the Arts at 623 West Main Street. Louisville Downtown Partnership lists the same artist, site, and address.

How is he posed?

The public-art record calls it a life-size bronze of Mayor Charles Farnsley seated on an oversized bench. Yates made the bench extra long because she thought Farnsley, a relaxed man, would have invited people to sit and talk.
