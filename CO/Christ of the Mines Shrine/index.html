Christ of the Mines Shrine in Silverton

Christ of the Mines Shrine is a 16-foot, 12-ton marble figure of Christ on the slope of Anvil Mountain, about 500 feet above Silverton, Colorado. The town asked for a shrine to its miners in January 1958, when mining was at its lowest ebb since Silverton began in 1874. The statue arrived in the summer of 1959 and was set on a pedestal of native stone. You can see it from town. The last stretch is a dirt road and, in spring, a short walk through snow.

The hike up, and the seasons

Reaching the shrine is a short climb, not a backcountry day. Lynn Arave's May 7, 2005 account in the Deseret News puts it above 10th and Bluff streets. A smooth, gradually climbing dirt road leads to it. Take Highway 550 north out of Silverton toward Ouray and take the first dirt road on the right, Shrine Road, only a few hundred yards along. Park about 600 yards later. From the car, a slight hike uphill finishes the walk. In spring that last bit may be snow. The figure is also easy to see from the streets below, so a storm day can still be a look from town.

The same account limits the car trip to late spring, summer, and early fall, and only on dry days. That is the season window to plan around. Silverton sits at 9,318 feet, high enough that snow lingers on a north-facing slope after the valley streets are clear. Do not count on driving Shrine Road in winter or on a wet day just because the highway is open. If the road is soft or still drifted, leave the car and walk the last stretch, or look up from town and come back when the dirt is dry.

Summer is the busy season in the valley as well as on the hill. Deseret News notes that the town, about 500 people year-round in that 2005 count, thrives in summer when the train from Durango arrives daily. The shrine is part of that season: visible as you come in on 550, and a short side road if you have dry weather and an hour. Countless visitors hike up, in the Catholic Travel Guide's description. Give yourself time to walk back down the same grade. The alcove is the shelter at the top. The view is the town in the valley and the mines that the figure was built to bless.

How the shrine was built

In January 1958 the Catholic men's club in Silverton talked about a shrine dedicated to miners. Mining was at its lowest ebb since the town started in 1874. They hoped a shrine might steady a depressed economy. In the spring, Father Joseph Halloran and a group from the club walked the slope of Anvil Mountain and chose it because anyone approaching Silverton would see a figure of that size looking over the town. Community donations paid for the work.

The alcove was to be native stone. The stone came from the Old Fisher Brewery and the land beside it, given by Mr. and Mrs. Ward Barlow. Work began in 1958. When the alcove was finished, a cutout stood in the niche for months while the town waited for the marble. The plan called for a figure 12 to 16 feet tall, of Carrara marble from Italy, the stone the Deseret News compares with Michelangelo's David. The statue arrived late in the summer of 1959. It is 16 feet tall and 12 tons. Father Halloran recruited the county crew and their equipment, and they set it on the pedestal.

Two bronze plaques are on the figure. The original reads: This shrine erected in honor of Christ of the Mines by the people of Silverton to ask God's blessing of the mining industry of the San Juans. A few months after the dedication, mining picked up and new tunnels were started. By the late 20th century the mines had faded again. The shrine stayed on the hill as the reminder of that industry, in a town that now lives on visitors as well as on what the mountains used to yield.

1978, the pines, and what the shrine is for

On June 4, 1978, a Sunday, a lake above the Sunnyside mine broke loose and flooded a lower portal. The Catholic Travel Guide names that lake Lake Emma and describes water pressure shooting mining equipment out of the portals. The mine was closed that day. There were no deaths or injuries. A second plaque, added in 1982, commemorates the lives saved. People in town have called that Sunday a miracle tied to the shrine. The reported facts are simpler and enough: the flood came on a day the workings were empty.

Father Joseph McGuiness, who followed Father Halloran, wanted trees behind the shrine so the bare slope would not stay bare. St. Patrick's Parish bought 1,000 Scotch pine seedlings and planted them. Silverton's garden season for ordinary plants was described as about 14 days, and plenty of people doubted the pines would live. A grove did grow. That planting is the other half of a visit: the marble figure in the stone niche, and the trees on the hill behind it.

The shrine is a miner's Christ, not a copy of the Christ of the Rockies near Grant. That other Colorado figure is a 33-foot statue above a private camp on the South Platte. This one is 16 feet of marble above a San Juan mining town, paid for by local donations, set by a county crew, and aimed at the road everyone uses to enter Silverton. St. Patrick's Catholic Church has been in town since 1882, in its present building since 1903. The First Congregational Church dates to 1876. The Baptist church and a Latter-day Saint branch were also in the town of a few hundred when the 2005 story was written. The shrine sits outside all of those buildings, on the mountain, where the mines were.

Read the first plaque for the purpose the builders wrote down: a blessing on the mining industry of the San Juans. Read the 1982 plaque for the Sunday in 1978 when the lake came down and the crews were not underground. Then look back at the town. The figure was meant to be large enough to see on the way in. If the road is dry, drive Shrine Road, park, and walk the last bit. If it is not, the hillside still shows the marble from town and from Highway 550.

Questions

Where is the Christ of the Mines Shrine?

On the slope of Anvil Mountain north of Silverton, Colorado, about 500 feet up from town and above 10th and Bluff streets. From Highway 550 toward Ouray, take the first dirt road on the right, Shrine Road, a few hundred yards out of town, and park about 600 yards later.

How tall is the Silverton statue?

The finished figure is 16 feet tall and weighs 12 tons. It is Italian marble. It arrived late in the summer of 1959, and a county crew set it on the pedestal in a native-stone alcove.

When can I drive or hike to the shrine?

The dirt road is described as suitable for cars in late spring, summer, and early fall on dry days. In spring, snow can still cover the last stretch, so a short hike uphill may be needed. The figure is also visible from town. Do not count on driving that road outside the dry-season window.

What happened at the Sunnyside mine in 1978?

On Sunday, June 4, 1978, a lake above the Sunnyside mine broke into the workings and flooded a lower portal. The mine was closed that day. There were no deaths or injuries. A plaque added in 1982 commemorates the lives saved.
