Pueblo History and Art Walk

The Pueblo History and Art Walk is a self-guided route through the Grove, Bojon Town, and Bessemer, three of the oldest neighborhoods in the city. A county map from February 2023 sets the loops, the art, and the historical stops.

Three neighborhoods, three loops

The walk is built for people on foot. It runs through three of Pueblo's oldest neighborhoods, with historical stops, art that rotates, and enough distance to count as a real outing. The loops are different lengths, so you can take one neighborhood or stay out for all three. Colored and numbered circles on the February 2023 county map match the stops along the street.

The Grove loop sits just south of downtown, the newest addition to the Pueblo Creative Corridor. Expect local art, one of the oldest churches in the city, and the Arkansas River at the edge of the walk. Bojon Town is part of the Grove and the place the revival started. Its landmark is the turnstile gate where steel-mill workers met their families at the end of a shift, and the loop also runs past Pueblo restaurants. Bessemer is the shopping loop: mom-and-pop stores on the Northern Avenue business corridor, time in Bessemer Park, and a football rivalry billed as the oldest west of the Mississippi.

Water Tower Place

On the Grove loop, Water Tower Place is the stop with a long industrial story. In 1891 Emmett Nuckolls and his son G. H. Nuckolls opened the first Pueblo plant of the Nuckolls Packing Company near the Union stockyards, about half a mile east of Bessemer Junction Station. In 1915 the family hired Hans Peter Henschien to design a packing plant on a rational-factory plan, so the product would move through the building like a machine. The plant is gone, the lot is a stop on the walk, and the site is being redeveloped.

The flood and the riverwalk

The Grove was an immigrant neighborhood. On one block you could hear Slovenian songs, and on the next you could see Italian flags. Then the Pueblo Flood of 3 June 1921 hit the lowest ground. The Grove sat close to the river and took the worst of the water. Most homes of the industrial workers were washed away. Survivors often moved up into Bojon Town. Others stayed and rebuilt. That is why the two loops belong together: Bojon Town is where many Grove families went when the river took the lower streets.

Finish the Grove loop at the Pueblo Riverwalk and you can follow the water back to your start. The photograph on this page is that canal, the concrete edge and the brick buildings. It shows the return leg, the riverwalk, rather than one of the numbered art stops. The Columbus monument elsewhere in Pueblo is a different outdoor figure and a different guide.

Give the walk a morning or an afternoon. One loop is a neighborhood. All three are a longer day of art, church fronts, the turnstile, Northern Avenue shops, and the river. Wear shoes for pavement, and keep the numbered circles in order so the packing plant, the flood streets, and the park stay in the sequence the map drew. Downtown Pueblo is the north edge of the Grove loop. When the canal appears, you are on the way back.

Questions

What are the three loops?

The Grove, south of downtown; Bojon Town, including the steel-mill turnstile; and Bessemer, along Northern Avenue and Bessemer Park.

What happened here in 1921?

On 3 June 1921 the flood hit the Grove hardest. The neighborhood was low and close to the river, and many homes were washed away. A lot of the survivors moved up to Bojon Town.

Is the photograph one of the art stops?

It shows the Pueblo Riverwalk. Completing the Grove loop brings you to that riverwalk, which leads back to the start.
