General William Jackson Palmer statue

The bronze equestrian of Gen. William Jackson Palmer stands in the middle of Nevada and Platte avenues in Colorado Springs, in front of Palmer High School. Nathan Dumont Potter made it. It was unveiled in 1929.

Plan your visit

The intersection of East Platte Avenue and North Nevada Avenue, just east of downtown Colorado Springs.

Stay on the sidewalk. The crossing is busy, and the bronze is only a short way out in the intersection.

In the middle of the crossing

The most familiar statue in Colorado Springs sits in the intersection of Nevada and Platte avenues. Traffic moves on all sides. A 1941 guidebook already placed a bronze equestrian of General William Jackson Palmer at East Platte and North Nevada, the work of Nathan D. Potter of New York City, unveiled in 1929. The same bronze is still there. Watch it from the sidewalk. The intersection stays busy, and the figure is only about forty feet from the curb. The photograph here shows the horse and rider with a Platte street sign at the edge and Palmer High School behind the pedestal.

The general

Palmer was born in Delaware in 1836. He was commissioned a colonel and later a brigadier general in the Union Army. He came to Colorado in 1869 as manager of construction on the Kansas Pacific Railroad. In 1870 he and his associates incorporated the narrow-gauge Denver and Rio Grande, developed coal and iron mines along the line, and founded the steel mills at Pueblo. Colorado Springs counts him as its founder, the Civil War officer who brought the railroad to this part of the West. He died in 1909 of injuries and paralysis after his horse stumbled on a ride near Glen Eyrie, his home at Garden of the Gods.

In 1929, years after that death, the General William Jackson Palmer Memorial Association installed the statue. Potter's name is given two ways: Nathan D. Potter in the 1941 guidebook, and Nathan Dumont Potter in later city writing. A file note on the photograph adds that the work was commissioned in 1924 and cast in 1929. The general looks toward Pikes Peak. People have long disagreed about the horse. Some say it is his beloved Diablo. Others say it is a representative horse, not a portrait of one animal. Either way, you are looking at Palmer in the saddle at the crossing he still watches.

How to spend the stop

Park at a nearby business lot and stay out of the lanes. The sidewalk view is the intended one. Read the pedestal, note the direction of the horse's head toward the peak, and leave the bronze in the intersection. Other Colorado stops on this site, including the history walk in Pueblo and Westminster City Park, are different cities. This one is a single equestrian in the middle of Nevada and Platte.

Palmer High School is the brick building behind the horse. The Platte Avenue sign is the cue that you are at the right crossing, just east of downtown. Nevada runs north and south here. If you are driving, the statue appears in the middle of the lanes before you have time to study it, which is why the sidewalk stop matters. A few minutes on the curb is the whole visit.

The life behind the bronze is the railroad and the city. From the Kansas Pacific work in 1869 to the Denver and Rio Grande in 1870, and the coal, iron, and Pueblo steel that followed the narrow-gauge line, Palmer's name is on the founding of Colorado Springs. Glen Eyrie, at Garden of the Gods, is the home where the riding accident happened in 1909. The association put the bronze in this intersection twenty years later. Potter's two recorded names, Nathan D. Potter and Nathan Dumont Potter, are the same New York sculptor. The 1924 commission and the 1929 casting are the dates to keep with the unveiling.

Questions

Where is the Palmer statue?

In the intersection of Nevada and Platte avenues in Colorado Springs, in front of Palmer High School.

Who made it, and when?

Nathan Dumont Potter, also recorded as Nathan D. Potter of New York. It was unveiled in 1929.

Can I walk up to it?

The crossing is busy. The practical view is from the sidewalk, a short distance from the bronze.
