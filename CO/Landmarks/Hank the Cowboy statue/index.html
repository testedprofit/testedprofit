Hank the Cowboy Statue in Colorado Springs

Hank the Cowboy is the bronze rodeo mascot in the median at East Pikes Peak Avenue and Tejon Street in downtown Colorado Springs. He reads the Colorado Springs Gazette-Telegraph and looks west toward Cascade Avenue and Pikes Peak. Rusty Phelps sculpted him. The Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo Association commissioned the figure, and the city received it in 1997.

The mascot and the bronze

Colorado Springs already had a Hank the Cowboy in 1939, decades before the bronze. Spencer Penrose, founder of The Broadmoor, asked Stanley Reed, an artist at the Colorado Springs Gazette-Telegraph, to draw a cowboy character for the Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo. That drawing is the mascot. The statue is the later, public version of the same figure, given to the city in 1997 so the rodeo's face would stand downtown all year, not only during rodeo week.

Rusty Phelps made the sculpture. The Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo Association commissioned it. El Pomar Foundation, the Anna Keesling Ackerman Trust, and the Gazette joined the support. The Gazette's May 4, 2026 account of the city's statues places him in the median at East Pikes Peak Avenue and Tejon Street, the crossing once known as downtown's Busy Corner, and a photograph in that story shows him there on April 27, 2026. He is still the paper-reading cowboy at that corner.

The clothes are a working outfit, not a parade costume. Jeans, chaps, a button-down shirt, a denim jacket, a cowboy hat, and boots. A can of chewing tobacco sits in a back pocket. The newspaper in his hands is the Colorado Springs Gazette-Telegraph, the paper whose artist drew him and whose name is still on the daily he holds. He looks west, toward Cascade Avenue and Pikes Peak, so the mountain is the view over his shoulder if you stand on the east side of the crossing.

Why a rodeo puts a cowboy on a corner

A rodeo mascot is a face the town can recognize before it recognizes a particular champion. The Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo needed a cowboy of its own in 1939, and Penrose went to the local paper's artist rather than borrowing a national cartoon. Reed's Hank belongs to that rodeo and to this city. The 1997 bronze moves him out of the program and onto the busiest old downtown corner, where people who never buy a rodeo ticket still meet him on the way across Tejon.

Other Hanks are easy to mix up with him, and they are not this statue. Western characters named Hank were popular in 1950s comics, as a puppet, and on the radio as Hank the Cowdog. Those came later. Colorado Springs had already named its rodeo cowboy Hank in 1939. If you came looking for the dog, or for a comic strip, you are at the wrong Hank. This one has chaps, a Gazette-Telegraph, and Pikes Peak in front of him.

He is also not the Cowboy in Pajamas in Denver, the painted bronze outside a downtown hotel there. That figure is a different artist, a different city, and a different joke. Hank is a straight working cowboy, commissioned by a rodeo association and given to Colorado Springs. Use the newspaper and the mountain to be sure you have the right one.

How to see him without standing in the street

The statue is in the median, which means the photograph is a crossing, not a plaza. Use the crosswalks at East Pikes Peak Avenue and Tejon Street. Wait for the light. Look from the corner or from the median refuge if the signal gives you one. Do not step into a travel lane to get under the hat. He is tall enough to read from the sidewalk: the open paper, the hat brim, the chaps, and the west-facing gaze.

On a clear day the backdrop is the reason for the pose. He looks toward Cascade Avenue and Pikes Peak, so a photo from the east side of the intersection can put the mountain behind the bronze. Morning light from the east lights his face. Later in the day he is more of a silhouette against the peak. Either hour works. The corner is called the old Busy Corner for a reason, so keep the visit short and stay with the signal.

If you are walking downtown statues in one loop, Hank is the Tejon and Pikes Peak stop. The statue of William Jackson Palmer is the rider at Nevada and Platte avenues, another median bronze in the same city, installed in 1929 and looking toward Pikes Peak as well. Palmer founded Colorado Springs. Hank advertises its rodeo. They are a few blocks apart and easy to confuse if you only remember a cowboy on a downtown corner. Palmer is on a horse. Hank is on foot, reading.

Other Colorado Springs figures

The same downtown holds more than these two. Starr Kempf's kinetic sculptures are the moving steel pieces associated with his Colorado Springs studio. Octo-Maid is another Springs figure on this site. Hank is the one you can find without a gallery: median, newspaper, mountain to the west, Tejon under your feet. The rodeo that named him still uses the Pikes Peak or Bust title. The bronze is the year-round version of that name.

Questions

Where is the Hank the Cowboy statue?

In the median at East Pikes Peak Avenue and Tejon Street in downtown Colorado Springs, the old Busy Corner. He looks west toward Cascade Avenue and Pikes Peak.

Who made the Hank statue, and when did the city receive it?

Stanley Reed drew the Hank character in 1939 for the Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo. Rusty Phelps sculpted the bronze. The Pikes Peak or Bust Rodeo Association commissioned it, and it was donated to the city in 1997.

What is Hank the Cowboy doing?

He is reading the Colorado Springs Gazette-Telegraph. He wears jeans, chaps, a button-down shirt, a denim jacket, a cowboy hat, and boots, with a can of chewing tobacco in a back pocket.

Is Hank the Cowboy the same as Hank the Cowdog?

No. Western characters named Hank showed up in 1950s comics, as a puppet, and on the radio as Hank the Cowdog. Colorado Springs already had its own Hank in 1939, made for the local rodeo. This bronze is that mascot.
