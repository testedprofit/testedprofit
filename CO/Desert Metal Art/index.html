Desert Metal Art in Grand Junction

Desert Metal Art is a metal yard-sculpture shop in downtown Grand Junction, Colorado, at 929 Pitkin Avenue. John and Jennifer Gallegos own it, on the lot where they used to run the used-car dealership Desert Auto. The bucking horses, the bull, the gorilla, the Bigfoot, and the velociraptor in the yard are merchandise, not a city monument.

From Desert Auto to a sculpture yard

Desert Metal Art grew out of a used-car lot. John and Jennifer Gallegos ran Desert Auto at this Pitkin Avenue address for years. In a June 3, 2026 Business Times story, John said they had been in the metal-art business for three years and in the automotive business for 20. Metal art started as a side job. By 2026 it had replaced the car business. He said the profit was not the same as car sales, and that the switch also fit a move toward retirement. The work is something they can keep doing.

The shop is at 929 Pitkin Avenue. That is the lot with the animals and the parking-lot entrance.

Jennifer Gallegos described one habit that survived the change of business. Customers still want to negotiate, the way they did for a used car. The shop listens. She said they try to keep prices as low as they can and still do all right. You can look without buying. John invites people to bring the kids and the rest of the family, walk the lot, and treat the yard like a museum. Every so often someone takes a piece home.

What the yard is for

The lot is a display of rusted-metal animals. A pair of bucking horses stands in that section. A large rusted bull and a gorilla, part rust and part shine, are paired nearby. At the parking-lot entrance, a tall rusty Bigfoot and a rusty velociraptor are the figures that pull drivers in. Those are the pieces the June 2026 story names. They are outdoor sculpture you can buy, cut and finished for a yard, not a single civic statue with a plaque.

The other half of the lot is plants that do not need water. John Gallegos said many customers in 2026 were skipping a garden and using metal flowers, metal cactus, and other metal pieces in place of a live plant. The reason he heard was water. People wanted to leave irrigation for farmers and ranchers, and they were moving yards toward xeriscape. A metal flower stays put in a dry Grand Valley summer. That is the practical education on this lot: sculpture as a substitute for a thirsty planting, in a desert city that already lives close to the edge of its water.

Grand Junction also has public metal art that is not for sale. The Dalton Trumbo statue is the 2007 bronze in a bathtub in front of the Avalon Theatre, part of the downtown Art on the Corner series. The Warren Barnes memorial is Tim Navin's iron reading man in a downtown breezeway. Those are city pieces you visit and leave. Desert Metal Art is the shop on Pitkin where the yard itself is the inventory. If you came for desert metal art in Grand Junction, start at 929 Pitkin Avenue, then walk downtown if you want the public bronzes too.

What kinds of pieces are in the yard

Think of the lot in two families. One is animals, mostly rusted steel. The pair of bucking horses is the group the June 2026 story puts in that animal section. Near them, a large bull in rust and a gorilla finished in both rust and bare shine make a pair. Those are big yard figures, the kind that read from the sidewalk and from a passing car. They are built to stand outdoors in a Grand Junction yard, not to sit on a shelf.

The entrance has its own pair. A tall rusty Bigfoot and a rusty velociraptor stand where cars turn in. John Gallegos treats them as the draw: people come to look, and every so often they buy something. If you only have five minutes, those two are the photograph. If you have twenty, walk past them into the animal section and then over to the plants.

The plant pieces are the practical half of the inventory. Metal flowers, metal cactus, and other metal stand-ins for a live planting. Customers told John they were skipping a garden for the year and using metal so the water could stay with farmers and ranchers. A flower that does not wilt is also a sculpture you can leave in a xeriscape bed through a hot summer. Sizes run from a single bloom you can carry to animals that need a truck. Ask which pieces are light enough for a car and which ones the shop would rather load for you.

Color splits the same way the story does. Much of the animal yard is the orange and brown of steel left to rust. The gorilla mixes that rust with brighter, bare metal, so it catches light differently from the bull beside it. The flowers are the pieces people choose when they want color in a bed that will not be watered. None of this is a city collection. Every figure has a price, and looking is the free part.

Parking and photo etiquette

Use the shop lot at 929 Pitkin Avenue. Leave the entrance open. The Bigfoot and the velociraptor stand at that entrance, so a car stopped in front of them blocks the next driver and blocks the picture everyone came for. Pull past the entrance figures into an open space, then walk back.

The yard is merchandise on display, and John has invited families to treat the walk like a museum visit. That invitation is to look. It is not an invitation to climb a horse, sit on the bull, or lean a child on the velociraptor for a portrait. Edges on cut steel are sharp, and a piece you tip can hit the next one. Stay on the walking space between the figures. If you want a flower or a cactus turned for a photo, ask. If another shopper is deciding on a piece, give them the angle and come back to it.

Photos of the lot are part of looking. A wide frame from the entrance shows the Bigfoot, the velociraptor, and the animal section behind them. Closer frames work for the horses, the bull and gorilla pair, and a single flower against the gravel. Keep people who are not in your group out of the close shots unless they are happy to be in them. Kids can walk the lot with you. Hold smaller hands near the tall pieces so nobody grabs a leg or a tail on the way by.

Best time of day for photos

Rust is a texture, and texture wants side light. On a clear day the useful windows are the first hour after the shop opens and the last hour before it closes. Weekdays that is about 10 to 11 a.m. and about 4 to 5 p.m. Saturday the listing runs 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., so the opening hour and the last hour are again the lower sun. Midday light sits higher and flattens the orange scale on a horse or the Bigfoot into one even tone. If the sky is bright and the only time you have is noon, shoot the gorilla, where bare metal next to rust still gives you contrast, and wait on the big rusted animals if you can come back later.

Stand so the sun is to your side, not straight behind the camera and not straight into the lens. The entrance figures are the easiest first test: if their edges throw a shadow on the gravel, you are in the good hour. If they look flat and gray-orange, you are in the hard midday light. Cloudy days are kinder to the whole lot at once, because the rust does not blow out and the shiny parts of the gorilla do not glare. After rain the colors deepen for a while. The lot is outdoors either way, so a hat and water still matter in a Grand Junction afternoon.

Call 970-216-4646 or 970-778-5151 if you are timing a visit to the last hour. The hours above are the ones on the shop's map listing. A short call tells you the gate is open and that a delivery is not filling the entrance while you want that photo.

How scrap metal sculpture is made

The animals and flowers on Pitkin are the finished outdoor pieces. The work that gets them there is ordinary metal fabrication, described here in general, the way published sculptors and welding engineers describe it. It is not a claim about one torch in the Grand Junction shop.

Scrap and recycled steel are the usual starting pile: sheet, bar, old tools, hardware, and other found metal. Shapes are cut out of that pile. Plasma arc cutting is a melting cut. An electric arc runs between an electrode in the torch and the metal itself. A nozzle squeezes that arc into a narrow jet of ionised gas, hot enough to melt the steel and blow the molten metal out of the kerf. The process cuts electrically conductive metals, including plain carbon steel and stainless steel, which is why it shows up in yard sculpture. The torch moves. The plate stays put. An angle grinder then takes off the burr and shapes an edge.

Joining is welding. MIG welding, a wire-feed weld, tacks the parts and then finishes the joint. An angle grinder cleans the bead. Scrap-metal work of this kind uses that trio together: welding, angle grinding, and plasma cutting, in protective leather, a welding helmet, and respiratory protection, because the work is hot, loud, and dirty. Hammering is the quieter part. The cut and the weld are most of the figure.

Finish is a choice. Some makers leave the steel to rust. Others galvanize it, then paint or stain the protected surface. That range is what you can see in this yard without a tour of the bench. The horses, the bull, the Bigfoot, and the velociraptor wear the rust. The gorilla keeps some bare shine next to it. The flowers are the colored pieces people put where a live plant would have been. Bare steel in a Grand Valley yard will keep changing color on its own. Ask the shop how a piece you like is meant to age before you put it in a bed.

None of that is a home project. The published warning from makers who do this work is simple: the torch, the plasma jet, and the grinder belong in a shop with the right gear. Your visit is the lot at 929 Pitkin Avenue, in the open, during the hours above.

How to shop the lot

Park past the entrance and walk. The Bigfoot and the velociraptor are first. The bucking horses, the bull, and the gorilla are the animal section. Flowers and cactus are the pieces people are choosing instead of live plants. Ask about price if you want a piece. Negotiation is part of the conversation, and the owners would rather keep the number reasonable than replay a car deal. If you only want to look, that is the visit John described: family, kids, a slow lap, and no obligation.

Questions

Is Desert Metal Art a public statue?

No. Desert Metal Art is a metal-sculpture shop in Grand Junction owned by John and Jennifer Gallegos. It sits at 929 Pitkin Avenue, on the lot that used to be their used-car dealership, Desert Auto. The animals, flowers, and cactus in the yard are for sale.

What will I see at Desert Metal Art?

A yard of rusted-metal animals, including a pair of bucking horses, a bull, and a gorilla. A tall rusty Bigfoot and a rusty velociraptor stand by the parking-lot entrance. The lot also has metal flowers and metal cactus meant to stand in for live plants.

How should I park and take photos?

Park in the shop lot and leave the entrance clear. The Bigfoot and the velociraptor stand there, so that is both the driveway and the first photo. Stay on the walking space, and do not climb or move a piece. Ask before you handle anything you might buy. The first hour after opening and the last hour before close give lower sun, which shows the rust better than midday.

When is Desert Metal Art open?

The shop listing on the map shows Monday through Friday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., and Saturday, 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. The phones on that listing are 970-216-4646 and 970-778-5151. Call before you go.
