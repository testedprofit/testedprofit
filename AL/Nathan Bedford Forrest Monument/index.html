Nathan Bedford Forrest monument

A monument to Nathan Bedford Forrest stands in Confederate Circle at Old Live Oak Cemetery, 110 Dallas Avenue in Selma. The first bust was unveiled in 2000, stolen in 2012, and replaced at a ceremony reported in May 2015.

The bust in Confederate Circle

Old Live Oak Cemetery sits at 110 Dallas Avenue, about seven-tenths of a mile west of downtown Selma. It was founded in 1829 and expanded in 1877. The newer ground is sometimes called New Live Oak, and the whole place is Live Oak Cemetery. Confederate leaders are buried here. So is Benjamin Sterling Turner, a formerly enslaved man who served as a U.S. representative from Alabama during Reconstruction. The Forrest monument is one object in that ground, a 2000 memorial in Confederate Circle.

The inscription, in part, calls him Defender of Selma, Wizard of the Saddle, Untutored Genius, and the First with the Most. It says the monument stands as a testament of perpetual devotion and respect for Lieutenant General Nathan Bedford Forrest, and it calls him one of the South's finest heroes. That is the stone's own language. The life behind the bust is wider than the inscription.

Who Forrest was

Nathan Bedford Forrest was a Confederate general, remembered for aggressive cavalry tactics and a rise from private to general in the Civil War. He was born into a poor family on 13 July 1821 in Chapel Hill, Tennessee. As a young man he made a fortune as a horse and cattle trader, a real-estate broker, and a cotton planter, and he worked directly in the interstate slave trade, including a slave jail in Memphis. He went into the Confederate army in June 1861.

After the war he was inaugurated as the first Grand Wizard of the Reconstruction-era Ku Klux Klan. He held that place for about a year. In January 1869, with the membership using methods he treated as ungovernable, he dissolved the Klan, ordered the costumes destroyed, and withdrew. The order had little effect. Few members destroyed their robes. He later denied that he had been a member. In the 1870s he twice spoke in favor of racial harmony. In his last years he sat on a railroad board and farmed President's Island with convict labor. He died of illness in 1877, at 56. The argument over the Selma bust, from the first unveiling on, has centered on that Klan role.

How this bust was replaced

The monument was first unveiled in 2000 at the Vaughan Smitherman Museum, where it was defaced. Within the next year it was moved to Confederate Circle, where it still stands. In 2012 cemetery workers found the bust missing and reported the theft to Selma police. The original was not recovered. The Friends of Forrest offered a $20,000 reward. On 23 May 2015 the Selma Times-Journal reported a second ceremony, held on a Saturday by the United Daughters of the Confederacy and the Friends of Forrest. A red curtain came off the new monument in front of a crowd of a hundred or more. The photograph here was taken on 30 May 2015, a week after that report, and it shows the replacement bust among the trees.

Come to 110 Dallas Avenue and ask the way to Confederate Circle if the paths are not obvious. You are looking for a bronze head on a pedestal, not a full standing figure. Read the inscription, and read it next to the life: cavalry general, slave trader, first Grand Wizard, and a dissolution order that failed to end the Klan. Questions

Where is the Forrest monument?

In Confederate Circle at Old Live Oak Cemetery, 110 Dallas Avenue, Selma.

Is the bust the original?

The 2000 bust was stolen in 2012 and was not recovered. The figure now on the monument is the replacement unveiled in 2015.

Why is the monument argued over?

Forrest was the Ku Klux Klan's first Grand Wizard. That role has been the center of the argument since the 2000 unveiling.
