Confederate Monument on Capitol Hill

The Confederate Monument rises 88 feet on the capitol grounds in Montgomery. It commemorates the 122,000 Alabamians who fought for the Confederacy, and it was dedicated on 7 December 1898.

Plan your visit

Address
Capitol Hill, at the Alabama State Capitol, 600 Dexter Avenue, Montgomery.
What to look for
A tall shaft with a bronze figure called Patriotism on top, a bronze battle relief around the column, and four granite soldiers at the base: Infantry, Artillery, Cavalry, and Navy.
On the lawn
The monument is an outdoor capitol-grounds stop. Pair it with a look at the capitol dome behind it.
Nearby
Court Square Fountain and the Hank Williams statue are downtown Montgomery walks from the capitol, not the same lawn.

What the monument commemorates

The monument on Capitol Hill was built to remember Alabamians who fought for the Confederacy, a figure given as 122,000. The Ladies Memorial Association of Montgomery carried the fundraising from the end of the war to the dedication, gathering $47,000 from events, private gifts, and appeals to the state. On 7 December 1898 the dedication included speeches before each granite figure was unveiled, and a tableau of 13 girls standing for the Confederate states.

The idea is older than the shaft. In November 1865 the Historical and Monumental Association of Alabama asked the legislature for $5,000 toward a marble monument. The next spring, pleas from Winchester, Virginia, about Alabama dead still in shallow graves pulled the effort toward cemeteries instead. On 16 April 1866 Montgomery women formed the Ladies Society for the Burial of Deceased Alabama Soldiers, soon renamed the Ladies Memorial Association. Sophie Lucy Ann Gilmer Bibb, who had run a soldiers' hospital in the city, chaired it. A May Day festival under her leadership raised more than $5,000 for Confederate graves at Richmond, Fredericksburg, and Spotsylvania in Virginia, Jonesboro and Resaca in Georgia, Franklin in Tennessee, and Corinth in Mississippi. In 1868 the association spent $700 on a marble obelisk and grave chart at Oakwood Cemetery in Montgomery, and from 1876 to 1882 it set granite stones on soldiers' graves there.

Who carved it

In 1882 the association turned back to a monument for Capitol Hill. In 1885 Mayor Warren Reese, a Confederate veteran, chaired a men's Historical and Monumental Society that contracted with the New York sculptor Alexander Doyle. The design put Jefferson Davis at the base of a shaft, with figures for the branches of service around him. Jefferson Davis laid the cornerstone on 29 April 1886 in front of about 5,000 people. The men then handed the lead, and $6,755 they had raised, back to the women's association.

The stepped base and the pedestals used Alabama limestone from T. L. Fossick's Rockwood quarry near Russellville. The tall shaft was a different stone. Conservation work later found that limestone came from Indiana, including Bedford, where Doyle's father owned a quarry, even though the association had described the monument as native Alabama stone. Doyle and the association fell out. He defended the contract and threatened suit as late as 1898.

The four standing figures came from another shop. In 1893 the association, through the Montgomery dealer Curbow and Clapp, ordered heroic granite from Frederick Barnicoat in Quincy, Massachusetts. Infantry, Artillery, Cavalry, and Navy were reported finished and accepted on 20 July 1898. Doyle's share of the finished monument is the bronze Patriotism at the top and the bronze battle scene wrapped around the column. Barnicoat's share is the four granite soldiers.

The flags in 2015

On 24 June 2015 Governor Robert Bentley ordered four Confederate flags removed from the monument, in the middle of a national argument over the flag in public places. The shaft, the bronze, and the granite figures stayed. The photograph on this page was taken in December 2010, so those flags are still on the poles. When you stand on the lawn now, you are looking at the 1898 monument without them.

How to see it

Come to the capitol lawn on Dexter Avenue and look for the tall column rather than the building's dome. Walk around the base so you can tell the four granite branches apart, then look up for Patriotism and the bronze band. The Hank Williams statue on Commerce Street and Court Square Fountain are the other Montgomery stops if you are making a day of downtown. The Richter memorial is on Maxwell Air Force Base and needs a pass.

Questions

How tall is the monument, and when was it dedicated?

It is 88 feet tall and was dedicated on 7 December 1898. Jefferson Davis laid the cornerstone on 29 April 1886.

Who made the figures?

Alexander Doyle made the bronze Patriotism and the battle relief. Frederick Barnicoat made the granite Infantry, Artillery, Cavalry, and Navy.

Are Confederate flags still on it?

Governor Robert Bentley ordered four Confederate flags removed on 24 June 2015. The column and the statues remained.
