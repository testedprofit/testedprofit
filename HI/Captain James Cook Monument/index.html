Captain Cook monument at Kealakekua Bay

The Captain Cook monument stands on the land at Kaʻawaloa, on Kealakekua Bay, Island of Hawaiʻi. The Division of Aquatic Resources says a 27-foot monument was erected there in 1878 near the place Cook was killed in 1779. You see it from Nāpōʻopoʻo across the bay, or you go to it on the Kaʻawaloa Trail or with a permitted kayak guide.

Plan your visit

Address
Kaʻawaloa Flats, Kealakekua Bay State Historical Park. Car access in the Division of Aquatic Resources directions ends at Nāpōʻopoʻo Beach: Highway 11 south from Kailua-Kona about 18 miles to the Nāpōʻopoʻo turnoff, then about 4 miles to the bay. The monument is across the bay from that beach.
Parking
The state pages describe the road ending at Nāpōʻopoʻo. They do not name a separate parking lot at the monument. The monument itself is reached on foot by the Kaʻawaloa Trail or by a permitted boat.
Hours
Daily, 7:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. No entrance fee.
Accessibility
Restrooms are only on the Nāpōʻopoʻo section of the park. The state parks page says there are no restrooms on the Kaʻawaloa Trail and asks hikers to pack out their trash. The pages do not describe the trail as a level paved walk.
Best time
The park is open from 7:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. From Nāpōʻopoʻo you can see the monument across the bay during those hours. A hike or a guided kayak needs the daylight inside that window.

History

The Division of State Parks calls the park the site of the first extensive contact between Hawaiians and Westerners, with Captain Cook’s arrival in 1779. From Nāpōʻopoʻo you can view Hikiau Heiau and the monument across the bay. The park page puts 4.0 acres in parentheses after that viewing sentence. Restrooms are only on the Nāpōʻopoʻo section.

The Division of Aquatic Resources says Cook arrived at Kealakekua Bay in January 1779 and was killed the following month in a skirmish on the shores of Kaʻawaloa Cove. In 1878 a 27-foot monument was erected in his honor by his countrymen near that place. The ruins of the ancient village of Kaʻawaloa are on the lava flats behind the monument.

The bay around the monument is a Marine Life Conservation District. The aquatic-resources page titles it HAR 13-29, 315 acres, established in 1969, on the western coast of Hawaiʻi near the village of Captain Cook, about 30 minutes south of Kailua-Kona. The district runs from the high-water mark seaward to a line from Cook Point to Manini Beach Point.

State parks prohibit permitted vessels from launching at Nāpōʻopoʻo wharf and from landing at Kaʻawaloa Flat. The beach side of the park is the place with restrooms, not a dock for a private landing at the monument.

The Kaʻawaloa Trail and the kayak rules

State parks give two ways onto Kaʻawaloa Flats, where the monument stands. One is to hike from the uplands on the historic Kaʻawaloa Trail. The page asks hikers to pack out their trash and says there are no restrooms on that trail. It does not print a mileage or a time. Plan water and a turnaround that gets you back inside the 7:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. park hours.

The other way is a guided kayak tour with a commercial vendor the Division of State Parks has permitted. The page says three permitted commercial vendors, then prints two names under the heading of authorized kayak tour companies: Adventures in Paradise, at 75-5660 Kopiko Street, C7-430, Kailua-Kona, and Kona Boys, Inc., at 79-7539 Mamalahoa Highway, Kealakekua. Those are the companies named on the page.

Individuals may cross the bay only in a vessel that already has a valid permit. The rule covers private boats and rentals, including kayaks, canoes, stand-up paddle boards, and inflatables. Permitted vessels may not land at Kaʻawaloa Flat and may not launch from Nāpōʻopoʻo wharf. A renter does not apply for a separate permit, but must confirm that the rented vessel already has one. Commercial kayak operators must hold a rental-vessel special-use permit, and each kayak needs a registered decal.

The Division of Aquatic Resources is blunt about cars. The only access by car is to Nāpōʻopoʻo Beach, along the bay’s eastern shoreline. Take Highway 11 south from Kailua-Kona to the Nāpōʻopoʻo turnoff, about 18 miles, turn, and go about 4 miles to the bay. Kaʻawaloa Cove, at the bay’s northern boundary, can be reached only by boat. From the beach the monument is the white shaft you see across the water, not a roadside pullout.

What to look for

The aquatic-resources page puts the ruins of Kaʻawaloa on the lava flats behind the monument. The height it prints is 27 feet, and the year it prints is 1878. From Nāpōʻopoʻo, the view across the bay takes in Hikiau Heiau and that monument.

Nearby sights

Hikiau Heiau is the other landmark the state parks page pairs with the view from Nāpōʻopoʻo. The village name Captain Cook, on the driving note, is the community on Highway 11. A different Captain Cook statue stands in Hofgaard Park at Waimea on Kauaʻi. That figure is a separate guide. It is not this monument and it is not on the Kaʻawaloa Trail.

Questions

How do you reach the Captain Cook monument?

State parks place the monument at Kaʻawaloa Flats. The two ways onto the flats are a hike on the historic Kaʻawaloa Trail from the uplands, or a guided kayak tour with a vendor the Division of State Parks has permitted. Permitted vessels may not land at Kaʻawaloa Flat or launch from Nāpōʻopoʻo wharf.

Can you drive to the monument?

The Division of Aquatic Resources says the only access by car is to Nāpōʻopoʻo Beach, on the bay's eastern shore. From Kailua-Kona, take Highway 11 south about 18 miles to the Nāpōʻopoʻo turnoff, then continue about 4 miles to the bay. Kaʻawaloa Cove, at the bay's northern boundary, can be reached only by boat. From Nāpōʻopoʻo the monument is across the bay.

How tall is the monument, and when was it put up?

The Division of Aquatic Resources says a 27-foot monument was erected in 1878 by Cook's countrymen near the place he was killed. The ruins of the old village of Kaʻawaloa are on the lava flats behind it.

What are the park hours, and is there a fee?

The state parks page lists daily hours of 7:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. and no entrance fee. Restrooms are only on the Nāpōʻopoʻo section. The Kaʻawaloa Trail has no restrooms, and hikers are asked to pack out their trash.
