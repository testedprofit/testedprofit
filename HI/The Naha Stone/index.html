The Naha Stone

The Naha Stone stands in front of the Hilo Public Library. Images of Old Hawaiʻi gives the address as 300 Waiānuenue Avenue, between Ululani and Kapiʻolani Street, and says the Naha Stone is the larger of the two stones there. The upright stone on the makai side is associated with Pinao Heiau. That page estimates the weight at two and one-half tons, which it also prints as 5,000 pounds. It says the stone was originally in the Wailua River on Kauai and was brought to Hilo by chief Makaliʻinuikualawaiea on his double canoe and placed in front of Pinao Heiau, a statement it attributes to the National Park Service.

Wikipedia estimates the weight between 2.5 and 3.5 tons. It says the stone originated from Mount Waialeale on Kauai, was found on the banks of the Wailua River, and was moved by double canoe to Hilo, where it became a symbol of the Naha rank. In the account on that page, an infant who stayed silent was treated as Naha rank, and an infant who cried was cast out. Only members of that rank could move the stone. A legend said that whoever overturned it would be granted the power to unify all of Hawaii. The page says Kamehameha was influenced by that legend, and that some sources say he moved the stone at age 14 after failed attempts. Wikipedia says the stone was relocated to the front of the Hilo Public Library in 1952.

The Honolulu Star-Bulletin of 14 November 2003 calls it the 3.5-ton Naha Stone. The story says vandals painted 1893-2004 on it, and painted a vertical white line on the smaller Pinao Stone, which once stood at the entrance of the nearby Pinao Temple. Hilo branch librarian Wilma Matsumura and Paul Neves, identified as alii aimoku of the Royal Order of Kamehameha I, are quoted. It says another legend has a young man of noble birth lifting the stone and fulfilling a prophecy to overthrow a mountain, and that the man became Kamehameha I. On the moves, it says the stones lay near the home of J.A. Scott on land now occupied by the library, were moved in 1916 several blocks to the old library, and were moved to the new facility shortly after it opened in 1951.

Those dates stay side by side: shortly after the new library opened in 1951, in the Star-Bulletin, and 1952 at the front of the library, on Wikipedia. The weights stay side by side as well: 2.5 tons or 5,000 pounds, a range of 2.5 to 3.5 tons, and 3.5 tons. The National Park Service frequently asked questions page for Puʻukoholā Heiau asks whether Kamehameha really lifted the 5,000-pound Naha Stone. The answer says the park was not there, and that Kamehameha became king of Hawaiʻi Island after he lifted the stone, which is what lifting it was supposed to guarantee. That sentence is the park’s wording. It is not a scale ticket at the Hilo library. The pages do not describe plaques in English and Hawaiian, and they do not list viewing hours.

Questions

How heavy is the Naha Stone?

Images of Old Hawaiʻi says two and one-half tons, or 5,000 pounds. Wikipedia says between 2.5 and 3.5 tons. The Star-Bulletin of 14 November 2003 says 3.5 tons. The Puʻukoholā frequently asked questions page also uses 5,000 pounds.

When did it move to the library?

The Star-Bulletin says the stones moved in 1916 to the old library and to the new facility shortly after it opened in 1951. Wikipedia says the stone was relocated to the front of the Hilo Public Library in 1952.
