How Bubble Cash works

Papaya's Bubble Cash page says players compete in tournaments, clear the board with combinations, and use boosters, challenges, and game modes. It also describes towns to build, cards to collect, and play with friends. The pitch on that page is to strategize your shots and turn bubble-bursting into cash wins. Support mail on the page is support@papaya.com.

Solitaire Cash is the sister app on the same site. Its page says you take on other players for real money prizes and win if you have the skills. It prints 40 million plus downloads and calls itself the leading solitaire game in the US. Michigan later named both apps in one letter.

Deposits, fees, and cash-outs

Michigan's Gaming Control Board said the Papaya apps let players buy in and win real money through gameplay. That buy-in is the deposit: you put money on a tournament. The Bubble Cash page describes the way out. You open your account, choose a withdrawal method, and the winnings transfer to that account. The page says the cash is processed quickly and securely.

Our own cash-out is narrower than a fee schedule. Tested June 24, 2025: we played, won a tournament, and withdrew to PayPal (a $1 processing fee was deducted). That $1 is what came off that PayPal withdrawal. It is the fee from this test, not a chart of every method Papaya offers.

Where Michigan drew the line

On October 3, 2024, the Michigan Gaming Control Board issued a cease-and-desist letter to Papaya Gaming in Tel Aviv. The board's release says an investigation, started from an anonymous tip, found Papaya offering 21 Cash, Bingo Cash, Bubble Cash, and Solitaire Cash in Michigan. The release says those offerings violate the Lawful Internet Gaming Act, the Michigan Gaming Control and Revenue Act, and the Michigan Penal Code. It says an unlicensed gambling operation is a felony punishable by imprisonment for up to 10 years or a fine of up to $100,000, or both. Papaya had 14 days from receipt of the letter to stop Michigan residents from gambling on the apps. Executive Director Henry Williams said illegal gambling is not tolerated in Michigan.

That letter is the state rule in the public record used here. It covers Michigan. It names Bubble Cash and Solitaire Cash together.

Skill, luck, and the court record

Papaya's pages talk about skill: strategize the bubble shots, and on Solitaire Cash, win if you have the skills. Judge Denise Cote's opinion in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York describes the market as real-money skill-based mobile games. The case is Skillz Platform Inc. v. Papaya Gaming, No. 24cv1646, against Papaya Gaming, Ltd. and Papaya Gaming, Inc.

Skillz filed the case on March 4, 2024. A jury trial concluded on April 23, 2026. The opinion rejects Papaya's motions to set the verdict aside and awards Skillz $719 million in disgorgement, plus attorney's fees for 2024 and 2025 and certain costs. The court declines prejudgment interest on that $719 million. The opinion says Skillz built the first platform of that kind in 2012, and that Papaya entered the market in 2019.

On the evidence at trial, taken in the light most favorable to Skillz, the opinion says Papaya filled tournaments with bots: scores designated by Papaya's algorithms, given usernames and profiles so they looked like customers. It describes liquidity bots that could make a 20-player tournament one human player and nineteen bots. A game sold as skill can still put you against software. That is the honest split between the marketing and the court record.
