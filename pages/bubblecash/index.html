Bubble Cash review

Bubble Cash is Papaya's real-money bubble shooter. The game page on papaya.com calls it a tournament game with cash prizes, and it prints 20 Million+ Downloads and the line Leading bubble game in the world.

What the game page says

The Bubble Cash page says players compete in tournaments, clear the board with combinations, and use boosters, challenges, and game modes. It also describes towns to build, cards to collect, and play with friends. On withdrawals, the page says you open your account, choose a withdrawal method, and the winnings transfer to that account.

Michigan cease-and-desist

On October 3, 2024, the Michigan Gaming Control Board issued a cease-and-desist letter to Papaya Gaming in Tel Aviv. The board's release says an investigation, started from an anonymous tip, found Papaya offering 21 Cash, Bingo Cash, Bubble Cash, and Solitaire Cash in Michigan. Those apps let players buy in and win real money through gameplay.

The release says the offerings violate the Lawful Internet Gaming Act, the Michigan Gaming Control and Revenue Act, and the Michigan Penal Code. It says an unlicensed gambling operation is a felony punishable by imprisonment for up to 10 years or a fine of up to $100,000, or both. Papaya had 14 days from receipt of the letter to stop Michigan residents from gambling on the apps. Executive Director Henry Williams said the board believes in a regulated and safe gaming environment and that illegal gambling is not tolerated in Michigan.

Federal case against Papaya

Judge Denise Cote's opinion in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York covers Papaya Gaming, Ltd. and Papaya Gaming, Inc., in Skillz Platform Inc. v. Papaya Gaming, No. 24cv1646. The Michigan letter names Bubble Cash among the apps.

Skillz filed the case on March 4, 2024. A jury trial concluded on April 23, 2026. The opinion rejects Papaya's motions to set the verdict aside and awards Skillz $719 million in disgorgement, plus attorney's fees for 2024 and 2025 and certain costs. The court declines prejudgment interest on that $719 million.

The opinion says the parties compete in real-money skill-based mobile games, that Skillz built the first platform of that kind in 2012, and that Papaya entered the market in 2019. On the evidence at trial, taken in the light most favorable to Skillz, the opinion says Papaya filled tournaments with bots: scores designated by Papaya's algorithms, given usernames and profiles so they looked like customers. It describes liquidity bots that could make a 20-player tournament one human player and nineteen bots.
