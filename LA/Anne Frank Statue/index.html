Founders Plaza, National WWII Museum

Anne Frank sculpture

A bronze of Anne Frank stands outdoors in Founders Plaza at the National WWII Museum in New Orleans, alongside Andrew Higgins Drive. StudioEIS made it. The museum unveiled it on 12 September 2019. This is an independent Profitnet guide to that memorial.

Visit

Sculpture
Founders Plaza, Andrew Higgins Drive between Magazine Street and Camp Street, New Orleans. The figure is outdoors, in front of the museum.
Museum
Drop-off at 525 Andrew Higgins Boulevard, in front of the Louisiana Memorial Pavilion. Galleries open daily, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Closed Mardi Gras Day, Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, and Christmas Day. Line (504) 528-1944.
Admission
General admission runs from $27 to $37 and covers the exhibit buildings. WWII veterans and one companion are admitted free. Active or former military and a spouse receive $10 off with proof of service. The museum is a private nonprofit, so a national-park pass is not admission.
Parking
Garage at 1024 Magazine Street. That block of Magazine is one-way. The garage is on the left.

The bronze and the bench

The figure is 5 feet 2 inches tall. Ivan Schwartz, who founded and runs StudioEIS in Brooklyn, called that his estimate of Anne's height at 13, when she went into hiding in Amsterdam in 1942 with her parents, her sister, and four other Jews. The bronze was based on photographs of her. Lead sculptor Jiwoong Cheh said the work took about a year.

She stands on the brick, without a pedestal, beside a trapezoid granite bench. Her face is turned up. She holds the diary to her chest. A seated bronze of Franklin D. Roosevelt is nearby. The bench was the second installation in the plaza. The Roosevelt figure and bench had been unveiled about two years earlier. Founders Plaza, dedicated in January 2017, crosses Andrew Higgins Drive between Camp and Magazine and is the museum's entry. Nuttall oaks line it. An 80-foot American flag and the six armed-forces flags stand there, along with sections of the Atlantic Wall and German sentry shelters. The Anne Frank bench and figure were placed to the right of the flagpole.

The side of the bench that faces the street carries a sentence from the diary:

Sometime this terrible war will be over. Surely the time will come when we are people again, and not just Jews.

A plaque on the angled end of the bench gives a short account of her life and the diary. The Oscar J. Tolmas Charitable Trust paid for the sculpture. At the unveiling, trustees Vincent J. Giardina and Lisa N. Romano lifted the cloth with Anne Levy, a Holocaust survivor and museum volunteer. The ceremony began at 10 a.m. beside the bench and continued in the Stage Door Canteen. It was open to the public.

The life the memorial marks

Annelies Marie Frank was born on 12 June 1929 in Frankfurt am Main, to Otto Frank and Edith Holländer. Her sister Margot was three years older. After Adolf Hitler became chancellor on 30 January 1933, the family left Germany. Otto moved to Amsterdam that summer. Edith and Margot followed. Anne, who had been with her grandmother in Aachen, joined them at the start of 1934. They lived in the Merwedeplein neighborhood. Anne learned Dutch and went to school, later to the Jewish Lyceum with Margot.

Germany invaded the Netherlands on 10 May 1940. Occupation rules then closed in: a yellow star, bans on streetcars and bicycles, a curfew, and separation from non-Jewish neighbors. For her 13th birthday, on 12 June 1942, Anne was given the diary she addressed to a friend she named Kitty. On 5 July 1942 Margot, then 16, received a call-up for a so-called labor camp. The family went into hiding the next day, in the annex behind Otto's business at Prinsengracht 263. Hermann, Auguste, and Peter van Pels joined them, and later the dentist Fritz Pfeffer. Eight people lived in the annex for a little more than two years. Helpers, among them Miep Gies, brought food and news.

On the morning of 4 August 1944, German and Dutch police raided the annex and arrested the eight people in hiding and two of the helpers. Anne, Margot, and Edith were sent on to Auschwitz-Birkenau. In November 1944 Anne and Margot were moved to Bergen-Belsen. Both died of typhus in February 1945. Anne was 15. Edith died in Auschwitz. Otto was the only one of the eight to survive the war.

Miep Gies had kept Anne's papers. When Otto returned to Amsterdam she gave them to him. He published the diary in Dutch in June 1947, under the title Het Achterhuis, in a first printing of 3,000 copies. An English edition followed in 1952. The Anne Frank House, which keeps the hiding place, counts later translations at around 70 languages. Otto stayed involved with that house until his death in 1980.

Inside the museum

The same trust that paid for the bronze also underwrote Holocaust exhibitions for the Liberation Pavilion. That building, the museum's last permanent exhibit hall, opened on 3 November 2023. Its first floor includes exhibits on the Holocaust and on Anne Frank, with the end of the war and the years after. Gallery tickets are separate from a walk up to the outdoor bronze. General admission includes the exhibit buildings and the USS Tang experience. Beyond All Boundaries and the Priddy Family Freedom Theater cost extra. The museum suggests buying tickets ahead. Fall and spring are the crowded seasons, and so is the week between Christmas and New Year's Day.

Questions

When was the Anne Frank sculpture unveiled?

The National WWII Museum unveiled the bronze on 12 September 2019 in Founders Plaza, alongside Andrew Higgins Drive in New Orleans.

Who made the sculpture, and how tall is it?

StudioEIS in Brooklyn made it. Ivan Schwartz leads the studio, and Jiwoong Cheh was the lead sculptor. The bronze is 5 feet 2 inches, Schwartz's estimate of Anne's height at 13, when she went into hiding.

Do you need a ticket to see the bronze?

The bronze stands outdoors in Founders Plaza on Andrew Higgins Drive. Tickets are for the museum galleries. The museum is open daily from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., except Mardi Gras Day, Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, and Christmas Day.
