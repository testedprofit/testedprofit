Huey Long Statue, Grave, and Plaque

The Huey Long statue is the grave marker at the center of the Louisiana State Capitol garden in Baton Rouge. Huey Pierce Long Jr. is buried beneath it. Charles Keck made the bronze, and the State of Louisiana set it up in 1940. The tombstone is the marble base: a plaque on the front, a longer plaque on the back, and reliefs of the programs he wanted remembered.

The statue, the grave, and the plaque

The Louisiana House of Representatives describes the capitol grounds as 27 acres of native plants, with oaks, magnolias, and azaleas. Two walkways, each twenty feet across, organize the lawn. A narrower path circles the outside, and smaller paths tie that circle to the main walks. Boxwood hedges border the paths. Camellias and azaleas line the inside of the main walks, and evergreens, juniper alternating with arborvitae, line the outside. The focal point of all of that is the statue of Governor and U.S. Senator Huey P. Long.

Because the capitol was his project, he is buried under the statue. The House dates the figure to 1940, put there to mark the spot. A 1995 Save Outdoor Sculpture survey, now in the Smithsonian's art inventories, names the sculptor Charles Keck, born in 1875 and dead in 1951, and classes the work as an outdoor sculpture and a gravestone. The bronze is a full-length portrait: Long standing in a double-breasted suit. It sits on a white marble base. That base rests on a granite foundation that forms the cap of the tomb. The photograph on this page is the garden view: the bronze from behind, the pedestal, and the tower over the hedges. Keck's signature, Charles Keck sc, is cut on the lower proper left of the sculpture and again on one side of the base.

The Huey Long plaque is the lettering on that base, not a separate sign down the walk. The front top reads Huey Pierce Long. The rear carries the dates and the epitaph. The 1995 survey records it in these words: Huey Pierce Long, 1893 to 1935, governor 1928 to 1932, U.S. senator 1932 to 1935. Here lies Louisiana's greatest son, Huey Pierce Long, an unconquered friend of the poor who dreamed of the day when the wealth of the land would be spread among all the people. Under that is a passage dated U.S. Senate, March 5, 1935, and signed Huey P. Long: he says he knows the hearts of the people because he has not colored his own, that he knows when he is right in his own conscience, and that he has one language whose simplicity should pardon his lack of letters. Fear will not change it, he says, and it cannot be changed while people suffer. The base closes with Erected by State of La. 1940. Walk around the pedestal and read the rear. That is the tombstone text.

Four reliefs share the marble with the plaque. On the east side, Long hands out free textbooks, parents with an infant look toward him, and three students in the background reach graduation. On the west side he meets architects, laborers, and craftsmen at the capitol and points to a model of the building. The rear relief is the building of the Huey P. Long Bridge across the Mississippi near New Orleans. The front relief is a riderless horse rearing, a leader slain, with a banner that reads Share the Wealth. The monument argues his case in pictures: schools, the tower behind you, the bridge, and the slogan, with the empty saddle for the death in 1935.

Who he was

Huey Pierce Long Jr. was born on August 30, 1893, in Winnfield, in the poor north of Louisiana. He worked as a traveling salesman, attended college only briefly, and was admitted to the bar. As a lawyer he often represented poor plaintiffs. He won a seat on the Louisiana Public Service Commission and used it against large corporations, including Standard Oil. He became governor in 1928 and a United States senator in 1932, and he kept control of state politics after he went to Washington. People called him the Kingfish.

The record of the governorship is both the building program and the fight over power. In 1929 the Louisiana House impeached him for abuses of power. The proceedings collapsed in the State Senate. Opponents said his policies and his methods were unconstitutional and authoritarian, and that he had made himself the political boss of the state. Supporters pointed at paved roads, bridges, hospitals, and schools, and at the free textbooks carved on this pedestal. Both accounts are part of why the statue still draws an argument. He is celebrated as a populist champion of the poor and denounced as a demagogue. The plaque on the grave uses only the first of those sentences. The garden does not erase the second.

In the Senate he argued that Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal did not go far enough. Share Our Wealth clubs spread his program, the same Share the Wealth named on the horse. He was preparing a run for president in 1936. Roosevelt later adopted many of his proposals in the Second New Deal. Louisiana elections stayed organized as pro-Long and anti-Long factions into the 1960s. His wife, Rose McConnell Long, followed him in the Senate.

September 1935, and the capitol he built

On Sunday, September 8, 1935, Long was at the capitol to pass a bill that redrew the district of an opponent, Judge Benjamin Pavy, who had held the bench for 28 years. At 9:20 p.m., just after the bill passed, Pavy's son-in-law, Carl Weiss, approached Long. The generally accepted account is that Weiss fired one shot from a few feet away and hit Long in the torso, and that Long's bodyguards then shot and killed Weiss. Long got down a flight of stairs, crossed the grounds, and was driven to Our Lady of the Lake Hospital. Surgery closed wounds in his intestines and did not stop the bleeding. He died at 4:10 a.m. on Tuesday, September 10, thirty-one hours after the shot. His allies said political opponents had arranged the killing. A federal probe found no evidence of a conspiracy.

More than 200,000 people came to Baton Rouge for the funeral on September 12. He was buried on the capitol grounds. The statue over that grave went up in 1940. The tower behind it was already his building. Construction of the new capitol took 14 months. It was dedicated on May 16, 1932, and Long missed the dedication because he was in Washington on Senate business. The House describes an Art Deco tower of 450 feet and 34 floors, the tallest state capitol in the United States, one of four skyscraper capitols and one of nine without a dome. It holds about 249,000 square feet and stands on these 27 acres. The statue faces the building he did not live to dedicate in person, on the lawn where he is buried.

How to read the stop

Come in daylight. The address for the capitol is 900 North Third Street. From the street, the tower is the landmark. On the grounds, take either of the twenty-foot walks toward the center. The bronze is the grave, not a statue inside the rotunda. There is no dome to confuse with the figure. Read the front name, walk to the rear plaque, then the four reliefs: textbooks, the capitol model, the bridge, and the riderless horse. One circuit of the pedestal is the visit. Oaks and magnolias shade the lawn. Azaleas and camellias edge the main walks.

The other Baton Rouge figure on this site is the Hebe statue, a different monument with its own history. Christ of the Rockies and the capitol statue are not the same kind of memorial: this one is a politician's tomb, with the state's own 1940 inscription choosing the words friend of the poor. Stand at the plaque and you are reading the case his monument makes. The impeachment, the collapsed trial, and the word demagogue are the case his opponents made, and they belong in the same conversation. The garden holds the bronze. It does not settle the argument.

Questions

Where is the Huey Long statue?

At the center of the Louisiana State Capitol garden in Baton Rouge, 900 North Third Street. Huey Pierce Long is buried beneath the statue. The grounds cover 27 acres.

Who made the statue, and when?

Charles Keck. The State of Louisiana erected the figure in 1940, five years after Long's death. His signature is cut into the bronze.

What does the Huey Long plaque say?

The front of the base reads Huey Pierce Long. The rear gives 1893 to 1935, governor 1928 to 1932, and U.S. senator 1932 to 1935, then calls him Louisiana's greatest son and an unconquered friend of the poor. A March 5, 1935 Senate passage is cut below that, and the base closes with Erected by State of La. 1940.

When was Huey Long killed?

He was shot inside the capitol at 9:20 p.m. on September 8, 1935, and he died at 4:10 a.m. on September 10. The funeral was on September 12, and he was buried on the capitol grounds. The generally accepted account is that Carl Weiss fired the shot and that Long's bodyguards then killed Weiss.
