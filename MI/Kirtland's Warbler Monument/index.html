Kirtland's warbler monument

On the Oscoda County courthouse lawn in Mio, along M-33, a stone-and-mortar monument holds the figure of a Kirtland's warbler. It honors the scientists, conservationists, and neighbors who kept the bird from disappearing.

The figure on the courthouse lawn

Most people driving M-33 through Mio pass the courthouse lawn without stopping. The lawn holds the usual military memorials, and among them an edifice of stone and mortar with the figure of a small songbird, brilliant yellow and slaty gray. The Michigan Department of Natural Resources describes it as a monument to the ingenuity and persistence of scientists, conservationists, and ordinary citizens who set out to save one of the state's most particular birds.

The photograph shows that bird in its stone niche. Use it to recognize the monument, then read the plaques on the wall. The stop is outdoors, on the courthouse grass, in the middle of town.

A bird that needs young pines

The Kirtland's warbler is small enough to hold in a cupped hand. Males and females both show lemon-yellow breasts and bellies, slaty gray heads and backs, and a scatter of black speckles. They nest on the ground in thick, patchy stands of young jack pine, and they spend the winter in the Caribbean. The scientific dates are tight: first discovered in the United States in 1851, wintering grounds discovered in 1879, nesting range discovered in 1903.

Logging in the 1800s opened the woods and let brown-headed cowbirds move in. Cowbirds lay their eggs in other birds' nests. When warblers raised those chicks as well as their own, the young warblers went without enough care to survive. Settlers also put out the fires that jack pine needs. Fire clears old pines, opens the cones, and lets the dense young stands grow back. Without fire, the nesting habitat aged out. By the time scientists looked hard at the bird in the 1940s, it was already in trouble.

From a thousand birds to a recovery

In 1951 counters walked the nesting grounds and tallied singing males, on the working assumption of one female for each male. The count came to 1,000 birds, and the nests were producing few young. The Michigan Department of Conservation, later the Department of Natural Resources, took the species on. By 1958, core areas were marked on state land in Ogemaw, Crawford, and Oscoda counties. By 1963 the U.S. Forest Service had set aside core areas of its own. Before the decade ended, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the warbler as endangered.

The work sat on a patchwork of state forest, national forest, and private land, so the coordination among agencies, researchers, and citizen scientists was the whole method. Numbers later rebounded. A 2015 conservation plan laid out how the partners would keep the bird from slipping back. Today the Kirtland's warbler is off the endangered list, and the population is over 4,500 birds in Michigan, Wisconsin, and Ontario. The Kirtland's Warbler Conservation Team still coordinates the habitat work, and the Kirtland's Warbler Alliance raises money and volunteers so the jack pine management continues.

Why the stone is in Mio

Oscoda County is one of the three counties where the state first drew those core areas. The courthouse lawn is the public face of that story, a bird in stone beside the war memorials, on the highway that runs through town. Stop, find the yellow figure, and you are looking at the local monument to a recovery that still depends on young pines. Other northern Michigan stops on this site, including the trout sculpture in Baldwin and Furtaw Field in Oscoda, are different towns. This one is the courthouse grass in Mio.
