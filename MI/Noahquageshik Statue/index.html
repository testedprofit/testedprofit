Noahquageshik statue

A seven-foot bronze of Noahquageshik, also called Chief Noonday, stands at Grand Valley State University's Eberhard Center in Grand Rapids, just west of the Blue Pedestrian Bridge. Antonio Tobias Mendez made it. The university unveiled it in 2010.

The bronze by the river

Grand Valley reported on 10 August 2010 that a new sculpture in Peter Secchia's Community Legends Project had been unveiled on the Pew Grand Rapids Campus. The Noahquageshik statue, also called Chief Noonday, was installed at the Eberhard Center, just west of the Blue Pedestrian Bridge, and it belongs to the university. It was the second of 25 sculptures meant for the city or a nonprofit, on a plan of one sculpture every two years for fifty years. President Thomas J. Haas thanked the Secchia family for a gift drawn from local Native history.

The bronze is seven feet tall and weighs 800 pounds. It sits on a three-foot-square base. Downtown Grand Rapids lists the same work as a 2010 bronze in the Community Legends series and gives the address 50 Front Avenue Southwest. Use the Eberhard Center and the bridge as the landmarks on foot, and the Front Avenue address if you are driving to the campus. The photograph shows him against the brick of the center, the name Noahquageshik cut into the base.

Who the plaque names

The plaque says Noahquageshik, about 1770 to 1840, was an influential leader of the Grand River Ottawa Anishinabe, the Original People. He led bands through the Grand River basin, including the Bowtink village that stood very near where the sculpture is now. The figure is the university's public portrait of that leader, at the river that the bands knew.

How Mendez made it

Antonio Tobias Mendez, of Knoxville, Maryland, has made more than twenty public monuments, among them Thurgood Marshall in Annapolis, Mohandas Gandhi in New York, and Arthur Vandenberg in Grand Rapids. For Chief Noonday he read historical documents and talked with the Native American community, the Grand Rapids Public Museum, the Ziibiwing Center of Anishinabe Culture and Lifeways in Mount Pleasant, and the National Museum of American History. He aimed for accuracy in the details that mark this region: the ceremonial pipe, and the split-feather headdress. Those are the things to look for when you stand in front of the bronze.

The Kirtland's warbler monument in Mio and the trout sculpture in Baldwin are other Michigan stops. This one is a riverfront figure on a university campus, a short walk from the Blue Bridge.

Walk from the Blue Pedestrian Bridge toward the Eberhard Center and look for the bronze on the river side of the building. The base carries the name Noahquageshik in large letters. The headdress is the split-feather form of this region, and the object in his hands is the ceremonial pipe Mendez treated as a required detail. The flowers and the brick behind him in the photograph are the campus setting, not a separate park.

Secchia's project planned twenty-five sculptures, one every two years for fifty years, given to the city or to a nonprofit. This was the second, and Grand Valley is the owner. Haas accepted it as a figure from local Native history. Mendez checked the likeness against the Grand Rapids Public Museum, the Ziibiwing Center in Mount Pleasant, the National Museum of American History, and people in the Native community, after reading the documents. The Bowtink village of the Grand River Ottawa stood very near this ground. The plaque's years, about 1770 to 1840, are the life you are standing in front of. Count the height against the doorway if you want a check: seven feet of bronze on a three-foot base.
